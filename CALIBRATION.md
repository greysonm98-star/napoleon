# Calibration check: d'Erlon's attack, Waterloo, 18 June 1815

One historical action run through the full model, to see where the simulation agrees
with the event and where it does not. Nothing in the data files was changed to make
it fit.

Scenario: `scenarios/derlon.json` (generated by `tools/gen-derlon.mjs`).
Reproduce: `npm run napsim -- batch scenarios/derlon.json --runs 100 --out out/derlon`
then `node tools/derlon-report.mjs out/derlon`. Every table below comes from
`tools/rerun-documented-numbers.sh`, run after the review fixes described in
`MODEL.md` section 11.5.

## The verdict in brief

- **The attack fails, as it did, but the test is a weak one.** The Allies still hold
  the crest at the end in 94% of runs. In all of those the result is awarded on the
  objective at the time limit or a stalemate; the French army as a whole never
  breaks, though all 17 of its battalions rout at some point.
- **The mechanism is wrong.** Historically the columns reached the crest, pushed
  back Bijlandt's brigade, were checked by Picton's infantry, and were then broken by
  the Union Brigade's charge. In the simulation two Allied batteries firing canister
  break most of the French battalions on the slope. Picton's two British brigades
  lose 3%.
- **French losses are too high.** 58% of the infantry, of which 21% prisoners (about
  1,900). The record suggests roughly 1,000 to 1,600 prisoners from these two
  divisions.
- **The Union Brigade is destroyed, which is worse than the record.** Eight of nine
  squadrons break; 23% are killed and wounded and most of the rest leave the field.
  Historically it lost somewhere near half and was spent. In the simulation the
  grand battery's canister does it; historically it was lancers and cuirassiers.

## What is modelled

The centre of the attack, at full battalion scale. The clock starts at 13:00.

| | Force | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| French infantry | Donzelot's division, 9 battalions, 5,130; Marcognet's, 8 battalions, 4,080 | In massed divisional columns: the `french_massed` doctrine, which takes 600 s to form square or deploy |
| French guns | Five batteries, 30 guns (two of 12-pdrs) | The share of the grand battery opposite these two divisions |
| French cavalry | 4 squadrons of cuirassiers (Travers), 5 of lancers (Jacquinot) | In reserve on either flank |
| Allied infantry on the crest | Bijlandt's brigade, 5 battalions, 2,400; conscript and militia quality | |
| Allied infantry behind it | Kempt's brigade, 4 battalions, 1,923; Pack's, 4 battalions, 1,707; veteran | 130 m behind the crest, out of sight of the French guns |
| Allied guns | Rogers's and Bijleveld's batteries, 12 guns | On the crest |
| Allied cavalry | Union Brigade, 9 squadrons, 1,260 | In reserve 350 m behind the crest |

Left out: Quiot's and Durutte's divisions, La Haye Sainte and its garrison,
Papelotte, the Household Brigade and the cuirassiers it met, Best's and Vincke's
brigades, and Picton's own fall.

All eight deeper-model features are on. Ground is mud; sky clear; light wind.

**Written orders.** French: the guns fire from 13:00; both divisions hold; at 13:25
the corps commander orders both to attack in column on the crest; the cavalry is in
reserve. Allied: defend; the Union Brigade is in reserve, with a standing order to
charge when the enemy comes within 120 m of Bijlandt's brigade.

**How sure the inputs are.** Strengths, positions and timings are from memory of
Adkin and Barbero, partly confirmed by web-search summaries (Donzelot about 5,100 to
5,300 in nine battalions, Marcognet about 4,000 to 4,200 in eight, a grand battery of
80 to 84 guns in all). The British battalion strengths, Bijlandt's 2,400 and the
Union Brigade's 1,260 were not confirmed. Treat every strength as within 10 to 15%.

## What happened, as far as I can establish

| | Historical | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Result | Attack repulsed; the French fell back to their start line | Certain |
| Timing | Bombardment from about 13:00; infantry stepped off between 13:20 and 14:00 depending on the account; cavalry charge about 14:15 to 14:30; over by about 15:00 | Sources conflict by half an hour |
| Bijlandt's brigade | Gave way as the columns reached the crest | Well attested; how completely is disputed |
| Picton's brigades | Fired and counter-charged at the crest; engaged, moderate loss | Well attested |
| French loss | 2,000 to 3,000 prisoners and two eagles from the corps of about 17,000 to 19,000; killed and wounded not found | Prisoner counts conflict; no total found |
| Union Brigade | Rode on to the grand battery, was counter-charged by lancers and cuirassiers, and lost somewhere around 40 to 50% | From memory; not confirmed |

Scaled to the two divisions modelled (about half the corps's infantry), the
prisoners would be roughly 1,000 to 1,600.

## What the simulation does

100 runs, seeds 1 to 100. Losses are medians.

| | Simulation | Historical | Agreement |
|---|---|---|---|
| Allies hold the crest at the end | 94% of runs, always on the objective rule; in the other 6% the Allied army breaks | Yes | Good, on a weak test |
| Timing | Ordered 13:25, in effect at the brigades about 13:34, first battalion breaks 13:49. Battalions rally and come on again; the last breaks about 14:44 | Stepped off 13:20 to 14:00, over by about 15:00 | Fair |
| French battalions that broke | 17 of 17 | Most of both divisions | Good |
| French infantry loss | 58% (10th to 90th percentile 42 to 68%): 31% killed and wounded, 21% prisoners, 6% dispersed | Not established; prisoners alone about 11 to 17% | Too high |
| French prisoners | About 1,900 | About 1,000 to 1,600 for these divisions | Too high |
| Bijlandt's brigade | 3 of 5 battalions break; 66% loss, mostly dispersed | Gave way | Fair |
| Kempt and Pack | 3% loss in the median run; 18% at the 90th percentile | Engaged at the crest | **Poor** |
| Union Brigade | 8 of 9 squadrons break; 23% killed and wounded; 90% lost to the brigade | About 40 to 50% loss; spent as a force | Worse than the record |
| French cavalry | 2% loss | Engaged against the British cavalry | **Poor** |
| What broke the French battalions | Canister 11.3 per run, melee 5.8, close musketry 0.6, charged while forming 0.6 | Musketry at the crest and the cavalry charge | **Poor** |

"Dispersed" means the unit left the field or fell below a tenth of its strength. It
is not a casualty figure. The Union Brigade's squadrons mostly rout northward off a
map that ends 250 m behind where they started, so "90% lost" overstates the men
actually hit.

## Where it diverges, and what that says about the model

**1. Canister decides the attack on the slope.** Two batteries break eleven
battalions a run. Each battery fires at one battalion until it routs, a few salvos,
then turns to the next. A battalion column breaks at about 16% loss to canister, and
the climb through the last 350 m takes about ten minutes in mud, long enough for the
batteries to fire every canister round they carry. The loss is concentrated on one
battalion at a time, with each battalion breaking on its own. Real batteries fired
into the mass in front of them, and a battalion inside a divisional column could
not easily leave it. This is the same sensitivity the Phase 2 sweeps found, here
with a historical case against it.

Halving and quartering the canister factor for both gun types (100 runs each):

| Canister factor | Allies hold the crest | Kempt and Pack loss | French routs by canister, by melee | French prisoners |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.1 (as shipped) | 94% | 3% | 11.3, 5.8 per run | 21% |
| 0.05 | 78% | 3% | 6.9, 7.4 | 27% |
| 0.025 | 51% | 14% | 3.7, 9.1 | 34% |

With less canister more columns reach the crest and the fight there decides more of
it, which is closer to the record. But Picton's brigades stay out of it until the
factor is a quarter of the shipped value, and French prisoners rise further from the
historical range. Lowering the canister factor alone does not reproduce the action,
so I have not changed it.

**2. Picton's infantry is a spectator.** A consequence of the first point, and of
the infantry standing on the reverse slope with nothing to bring it forward until a
brigadier's initiative fires.

**3. The cavalry charge is not the decisive blow.** The Union Brigade does charge
(on Ponsonby's initiative or on the standing order) and does ride down battalions
caught unable to form square; the gap in prisoners between the two formations
below suggests that is where many of them come from. But most battalions have
already been broken once by the guns.

**4. The French cavalry does little.** The British squadrons are broken by the grand
battery's canister before the lancers reach them. In the model cavalry prefers guns
as a target, charges them frontally, and blown cavalry sits in front of the battery.
The overreach is reproduced; the counter-charge is not.

**5. No shot passes through one unit into the next.** A deep mass is no worse a
target for roundshot than a single battalion column, so the bombardment does little.

**6. Routed battalions come back.** French battalions rally in the valley and climb
the slope again, so the last one breaks an hour after the first. D'Erlon's corps
took the rest of the afternoon to re-form.

## The massed column

The histories blame the formation: battalions deployed in line and stacked a few
paces apart, unable to form square. The same scenario with ordinary battalion columns
(`scenarios/derlon-ordinary-columns.json`, 100 runs, same seeds):

| | Massed columns (historical) | Ordinary columns |
|---|---|---|
| Allies hold the crest | 94% | 86% |
| French infantry loss | 58% | 33% |
| of which prisoners | 21% | 6% |
| French battalions broken while forming, per run | 0.6 | 0.1 |
| Kempt and Pack loss | 3% | 18% |
| Union Brigade squadrons broken | 8 of 9 | 7 of 9 |

In ordinary columns the battalions form square in 45 seconds and the cavalry recoils
from them; in the massed formation they are caught, prisoners more than triple, and
the attack does worse.

This is not independent confirmation of the standard account. The massed formation
is represented by one number, a 600-second time to form square, which I chose to
mean "cannot form square in time". The table shows what follows in the model if
that is true; it does not show that it was true.

## Which features matter here

Each feature switched off in turn (historical scenario, 100 runs each). With all on:
crest held 94%, French infantry loss 58%, Bijlandt 66%, Kempt and Pack 3%.

| Switched off | Crest held | Most visible change |
|---|---|---|
| Command and control | 18% | With no orders to wait for, everything attacks at once from the first minute, cavalry included, and the Allies are swamped in 25 minutes |
| Weather and ground (firm, not mud) | 67% | The attack arrives faster and Bijlandt's whole brigade breaks (86% loss) |
| Skirmishers | 87% | French loss 54% |
| Smoke | 88% | French loss 55% |
| Line of sight | 92% | French loss 49% |
| Fatigue | 93% | French loss 63% |
| Ammunition | 93% | Kempt and Pack lose 21%: the firing never stops |
| Quality | 96% | Little |

With 100 runs, a share near 90% has a 95% interval of roughly 83% to 95%. Only the
command-and-control and ground rows are clearly different from the baseline; the
other six are not distinguishable from it on this evidence.

Command and control changes the result more than everything else together, because
it is what holds the attack back for the bombardment and keeps the reserves in
reserve. Mud is the other clear effect: on firm ground the attack succeeds a third
of the time.

## What I would look at next

In order of how much the evidence above points at them:

1. How artillery chooses and holds targets, and how a battalion inside a larger mass
   shares loss and morale with its neighbours.
2. Rallying: how soon a beaten battalion is fit to attack again.
3. The canister factor, tested against a second historical action before it is changed.
4. Roundshot passing through to units behind.
5. Cavalry's preference for charging guns frontally, and what blown cavalry does next.
6. A rule that brings reverse-slope infantry forward to the crest when the enemy nears it.
